<CaseSection title="Designing for minutes of training">
  <Prose>
    The people selling are volunteers and temporary staff, and most of them learn the ticketing rules minutes before they start. That one constraint shaped everything: clear language, one decision per screen, and real-time feedback so nobody has to guess whether a sale went through.

    <PullQuote>Good design isn't about screens — it's about the people using them.</PullQuote>
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Branding that flexes">
  <Prose>
    The same app runs a stadium game one weekend and a hospital fundraiser the next. I introduced variables and components so marketing and production can restyle it for each context — from sports arenas to charity galas — without pulling in a developer.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Approach">
  <Prose>
    I ran internal interviews to map where operators actually struggled, designed one-action-per-screen flows to cut errors under pressure, and ran shadowing sessions during field testing — watching brand-new users in a real crowd surfaces the gaps you never catch at a desk.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="A chat feature for the floor">
  <Prose>
    Communication is critical during high-traffic events, where every second counts. Raffle managers can push instructions to every operator at once or target a specific group, which keeps tasks moving and issues short-lived.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Less paper, fewer delays">
  <Prose>
    Printing six to seven hundred ticket numbers per event meant delays and waste. Adding a QR code to each ticket let participants check winning numbers on their phone — better for the staff running the table, and better for the crowd waiting on a result.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>
