<CaseSection title="Context">
  <Prose>
    Ascend's business was built on running seasonal raffles and campaigns for over 500 charities, sports teams, and hospitals. ChangeIt was its initiative to build monthly recurring revenue (MRR).

    The product started on the charity side. Donors linked a credit card, rounded up their purchases, and received benefits in return. Midway through 2024, Ascend saw an opportunity in US college sports and pivoted ChangeIt to a membership model. Members were mostly former students, now parents, who wanted the best for their school and its teams.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<Credits />

<CaseSection title="Problem">
  <Prose>
    The round-up product was built for online sign-ups. Users signed up for free, explored the benefits, and only then decided whether to link a credit card. The idea was to show value before the step with the most friction.

    In the new market, that front door was broken. Most members signed up at game-day stands with a line waiting, and sign-up took about six minutes. Only about 12% of people who started became members, and 76% dropped off at payment. Every lost minute meant lost members and lost MRR.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Approach">
  <Prose>
    Once sales moved to game-day stands, we had data to work from. I had created the tracking plan, defined the naming conventions, and tied the metrics to user and business goals, so we could see exactly where people dropped off.

    But analytics only showed what was happening, not why. To close that gap, I asked our team at the stands to record sign-up sessions with real users. The recordings revealed three problems:
  </Prose>

  <Findings>
    <Finding number="Finding 01" title="Sign-in method">In this setting, people preferred to sign in with their own email rather than a social account.</Finding>
    <Finding number="Finding 02" title="Length">The multi-step sign-up was too long for a stand with a line waiting.</Finding>
    <Finding number="Finding 03" title="Payment timing">Because payment options appeared only after the benefits, many people left and never came back.</Finding>
  </Findings>

  <Callout headline="Every change had to earn its place.">We were still validating the new market, so we couldn't afford to disrupt the whole product.</Callout>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Design decisions">
  <Prose>
    The recordings and the funnel data pointed to three moments in sign-up. I weighed each one against how many people we were losing there, and kept every change targeted, since we were still validating the market.
  </Prose>

  <ApproachItem number="01" title="Remove doubt at the moment of payment" spaceBefore="24px">
    <Stat>76%</Stat> of people who reached payment never became members, and <Stat>25%</Stat> went back to the tiers page. The third-party payment form sometimes loaded as a blank space, which made the flow feel broken even when it worked. Even people who converted took a median of <Stat>2 min 46 s</Stat> on this step alone.

    <Decision>We couldn't change the third-party form itself, so I designed around it: a clear loading state, tier details kept visible inside billing, and a way to change tiers without losing progress. Now, everything in one page, different from the first version that was splited in different steps. This give time to load the third-party form and provide all feedback in one page.</Decision>
  </ApproachItem>

  <ApproachItem number="02" title="Show the offer before asking for commitment">
    The tiers page was the biggest drop-off before payment. <Stat>57%</Stat> of users left before reaching billing, because we were asking them to create an account before they understood the offer or the price.

    <Decision>Move tier selection ahead of account creation, with easier-to-scan tier cards and a clearer select button. We didn't control every client's landing page, so I designed a fallback for cases where a tier couldn't be passed into sign-up. The ideal path worked without breaking the others.</Decision>
  </ApproachItem>

  <ApproachItem number="03" title="Lead with the path people actually chose">
    The original flow put social login first, but <Stat>55%</Stat> of users chose email. The sign-up page had a <Stat>33%</Stat> bounce rate, which was worth fixing.

    <Decision>Make email the primary path, with email and password on the first screen, and remove the extra account info step.</Decision>
  </ApproachItem>
</CaseSection>

<CaseSection title="Learning">
  <Prose>
    The biggest lesson was that different contexts need different approaches. Showing value before asking for payment is a sound pattern online, but at a game-day stand with a line waiting, it became friction. The fixes that worked were designed around what we couldn't control, like the third-party payment form and client landing pages. It showed me that the user experience often starts before people reach your product, and that it's important to design around constraints, not against them.

    ChangeIt closed at the end of 2025. It depended heavily on each client and faced changing regulations on how schools and athletes could raise donations, which made it hard to scale. If I did it again, I would push harder for user research before committing to the market, and move to a checkout-only product sooner.
  </Prose>
</CaseSection>
